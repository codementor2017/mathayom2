4.1 The Map ⏱️ 0–10 min

Look at a detailed photo of a city. Now look at a simple subway map.

Which is easier to use for finding a route? The subway map.

Why? Because it uses abstraction. It focuses on what matters for the goal (stations and lines) and ignores what does not (trees, buildings, people).

🗣️ Think–Pair–Share (2 minutes):

Have you ever used a map that was too detailed? What happened? Why was it hard to use?

4.2 Abstraction as Modeling ⏱️ 10–25 min

Abstraction is removing unnecessary details to focus on the important ones.

A model is a simplified representation of a problem.

  • A map is a model.
  • A diagram is a model.
  • A recipe is a model.

Examples:

  • Google Maps: Shows roads but not trees.
  • A recipe: Shows ingredients and steps but not the chef's name.
  • A class schedule: Shows time and subject but not the color of the room.
👩‍🏫 Tip: Show a real recipe and ask students: "What has been abstracted away?" (e.g., the chef's name, the color of the bowl, the music playing in the kitchen.)

4.3 Abstracting the Height-Lining Problem ⏱️ 25–30 min

Problem: Line up students by height as fast as possible.

✅ Essential details

  • Height
  • Order (shortest to tallest)

❌ Non-essential details

  • Hair color
  • Shirt color
  • Shoe size

🎯 Model

A line with positions numbered 1 to N.

4.4 Abstracting the Shirt Organization Problem ⏱️ 30–45 min

Problem: Arrange shirts so you can find them easily.

✅ Essential details

  • Type (T-shirt, long-sleeve)
  • Color
  • Frequency of use

❌ Non-essential details

  • Brand
  • Price
  • Who bought it

🎯 Model

A grid with rows for type and columns for color.

🗣️ Quick Check (2 minutes):

Why is "brand" considered non-essential? Could it ever become essential? Discuss with your neighbor.

☕ Break Time ⏱️ 45–55 min

Stretch, hydrate, and reset your brain. When you return, you'll create your own abstract model! 🦁

4.5 Group Activity: Abstract a Problem ⏱️ 55–80 min

Work with a partner. Choose one problem:

  • Arrange a bookshelf for easy finding.
  • Organize a kitchen for fast cooking.
  • Design a study schedule for exam week.
  • Arrange a playlist for different moods.
  • Organize a toolbox for quick repair.

Your task — identify:

  1. Essential details.
  2. Non-essential details.
  3. A model (diagram, grid, flowchart) that represents the problem.

Then draw the model on a poster. Use colors, labels, and clear icons.

👥 Suggested Group Roles

  • Thinker: decides what is essential vs non-essential.
  • Designer: draws the model (grid, diagram, flowchart).
  • Presenter: explains the abstraction to the class.
  • Timekeeper: keeps the group on schedule.
👩‍🏫 Tip: Remind students: abstraction is not about removing ALL details — it's about removing the RIGHT details for the goal. Ask: "What do we need to solve THIS problem?"

4.5b Gallery Walk & Presentations ⏱️ 80–95 min

Each group will display their model. Walk around and look at other groups' work.

As you visit each model, ask yourself:

  • Did they identify the essential details clearly?
  • Did they remove the right non-essential details?
  • Is their model easy to understand?
  • Does the model help solve the problem?

Then each group's Presenter gives a 2-minute explanation of their model to the class.

🗣️ Peer Feedback:

After each presentation, give one star (something you liked) and one wish (something that could be improved).

4.6 Chapter Summary ⏱️ 95–97 min

  • Abstraction means removing unnecessary details to focus on the important ones.
  • A model is a simplified representation of a problem.
  • Models help us understand and solve problems.
  • Abstraction makes complex problems easier to handle.

4.8 Practice & Exit Ticket ⏱️ 97–100 min

Homework: Abstract your wardrobe. Create a model (diagram or grid) for organizing it.

🎟️ Exit Ticket — Complete before you leave

In one sentence, explain what abstraction is.

Then list one essential and one non-essential detail for this problem: "Choose a movie to watch tonight."
